I do not support it, and I said so on the floor, from memory of that debate as I recall it. I moved that the executive have no salary beyond his expenses. My reasons were these. Two passions govern most men's affairs, the love of power and the love of money. Join them in one office, and I apprehend you give the whole force of both to the man who holds it, and the places of honour will be sought by the ambitious and the greedy rather than by the fit.

I pointed to our own experience. We had officers in the late war who served without pay, and men sat in our public bodies and hospitals who asked nothing for it. It is a mistake to think that none will serve unless paid; the best men serve for the honour of it, and a place that rewards mere profit draws the other sort. I did not mean it only for the first man to hold the post, for whom we all had confidence, but for those who would follow.

I must confess that my motion was seconded, and was treated with respect, and then was let drop. I think the members did not wish to contradict an old man who had been useful to them, and did not wish to take his advice either. I conceive they feared that only the rich would then sit in that chair, and there is something in that. If the office must be paid, let the sum be fixed, so that the holder cannot raise it by favours, and let it never be a prize worth scheming for. That was my whole argument, and I still think it a fair one.
