Mr. President, I confess I am not of the opinion of those who think the executive should serve without pay. It sounds a generous principle, that the office should be filled by men of ample fortune who will ask nothing, and I have heard it urged with great warmth. But I have observed in the course of a long life that the best of men may be corrupted by a love of power and a love of money, and where the two are joined in one office there is nothing so apt to make mischief as a post of honour that is at the same time a place of profit. Yet it does not follow that the answer is no salary.

My concern is the contrary one. If we say the office shall be unpaid, we shall in effect declare that only the rich may hold it. A poor man of great ability is shut out, and the rich man who serves for nothing will soon find ways to be repaid, by favours, by friends placed in office, by the quiet management of contracts. Better to pay a modest, fixed sum openly, settled by law, so that the public knows what the servant costs and can see what he receives. I would fix it and forbid any other emolument whatever, so that the temptation of gain is removed by being made plain.

I would add, Sir, that I have little fear that the post will lack candidates. Our history shows that the struggle for power and for profit has produced more quarrels among mankind than any other cause, and a nation that makes an office both lucrative and powerful will be plagued with intriguers. Let the salary be sufficient for dignity and no more. A people who may remove a chief magistrate in an orderly way, by election and, if need be, by impeachment, will be better served than by one they can be rid of only by assassination.
