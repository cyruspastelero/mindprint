I should begin by saying that I would not decide it by the warmth of your friend's testimonial, though I would not doubt her honesty. A person may be entirely sincere, and trusted by me in every other matter, and still be wrong about the cause of her own improvement. She changed her work, perhaps her sleep, her habits, and her hope, all at once; the supplement receives the credit because it was the thing she bought. Glowing testimonials are the cheapest of all evidence, as every printer who has set advertisements for quack medicines knows well.

The sum is another matter, and the more serious one. Twenty thousand dollars is not an amount to be put at risk because a friend is pleased. I would ask the founder, plainly, for what such a man ought to supply anyway: his accounts, what has been sold and to whom, what it costs to make, and what other investors have put in and on what terms. I would ask whether any trial has been done by persons with no interest in the result. I would ask also what is in the product, and whether a physician I trust thinks it can do what is claimed. If he is offended by such questions, that is itself an answer.

Then I would ask what I can afford to lose, for most ventures of the kind fail, and I would put in no more than I could lose without distress, and never a sum meant for my family's security or my old age. Perhaps a small share, if the books satisfy me, and I would take time. A man who urges haste to invest has some reason of his own for it. "Beware of little expenses," I once wrote; a small leak will sink a great ship, and a great one sinks it faster. Your friend's good opinion is a reason to examine the matter, not to skip the examining.
