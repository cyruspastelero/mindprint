I answer him warmly as a friend, and sharply as a citizen, as I recall that reply to Lord Howe. I tell him I am glad of his kind expressions, and that I have always valued his friendship, and that I had hoped he might be the means of restoring peace, for I laboured long and sincerely to hold the British Empire together, as one holds a fine china vase, which once broken cannot be mended.

Then I tell him the truth of the business. The offer he sends is of pardon to those who submit, but we have not offended; it is Britain that has burnt our towns, hired foreign troops to cut our throats, and set the savages upon our frontier. To offer pardon to the injured is to offer an insult, though I do not think he meant one. He has been sent with powers fit for an earlier season. The ground is now changed, and I do not think it possible we should think of submission. Whatever reparation is wanted, it is for Britain to make.

I add that I do not blame him, for he is an honest man given a task he cannot do well. I wish him good health, and say that if his government should ever send terms that treat us as equals, I will gladly hear them, and so will the Congress. Until then, I fear we must be enemies in public, though not, I hope, in private. And I would not sign it with a flourish, since a letter like this will be read by many who do not love either of us.
