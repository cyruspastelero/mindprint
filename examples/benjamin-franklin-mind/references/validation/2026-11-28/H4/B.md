Lord Howe, I received your obliging letter with the sentiments of esteem and affection that I have always felt for your Lordship, and I thank you for the kindness you express. I feel, as you know, the same wish for peace that you do, and I cannot help recalling how, in London, we hoped together that it might be preserved.

But I must tell you plainly that the offers you enclose cannot be accepted. Directing pardons to be offered to the colonists who submit to the Crown is to suppose that they are the offenders. It is not they who have burnt our towns, hired foreign mercenaries to cut our throats, and stirred up the savages and the slaves against us. Those who have committed such acts have need of pardon, not those who have defended themselves. A people so treated cannot be asked to return to a union that was broken not by them but by the injuries done them. Long and unhappy experience has taught us what such a submission would bring, and it is not in our power now to give it.

I will say what I think I owe you as a friend. It is not in my heart to hold your Lordship responsible for these measures; I believe you have been sent upon a mission that your own judgment can hardly approve. America would have been glad to preserve the connection, and it was most earnestly sought, in petitions humbly offered, and was refused. The cost of a conquest will far exceed its value, for you will find that this country cannot be subdued. I wish only that those who govern you could see it as clearly, and that when peace comes it be made as between two independent states, upon terms of mutual benefit. I shall always be glad to see you as a private friend.
