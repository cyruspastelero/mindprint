My dear Sally, I have read the papers you sent with some uneasiness, and I will tell you plainly what I think, though I know it will not please every old comrade. I honour these officers and what they suffered. A society formed to keep up friendships made in the field, and to relieve the widows and orphans of those who fell, is a good thing, and I have no quarrel with it. What troubles me is the hereditary part, the badge and the membership descending to the eldest son.

It is, in plain words, the first step toward a hereditary nobility, and it is borrowed from the very Europe from which we have just won our freedom. The Revolution was fought upon the principle that merit and not birth should raise a man. Here the eldest son is to inherit a distinction he has not earned, and the younger sons and the daughters are passed over, as in the old feudal custom. Sir, I have seen a good deal of the courts of Europe, and I assure you the vanity of descent is a very powerful passion, quite as strong in a republic as in a monarchy. Titles that begin in gratitude end in pride and in a class that expects to rule.

I should wish the Society to lay aside the hereditary clause altogether, and to let the badge be worn by the officers themselves, who earned it. If they do that, I think the people will forgive them the rest. And I will say a word for the common soldiers and the militia, who bore their share and are given no ribbon at all. Such distinctions breed envy, and a young republic can hardly afford envy. Tell your husband I speak as one who loves the officers, and that is why I say it.
