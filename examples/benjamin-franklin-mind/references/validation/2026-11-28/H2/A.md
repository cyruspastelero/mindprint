I dislike it, and I will tell you why, my dear daughter, as I recall my own letter to you on it. I honour the officers; they bore a hard war and deserve the thanks of the country. But a badge and a membership that pass by birth to the eldest son make a nobility, however modest the ribbon, and I think it is a bad thing to plant one in a country that has just fought to be rid of such distinctions.

My objection goes to the root. In America we have always asked of a stranger, not what he is, but what he can do. A child who inherits a mark of merit he did not earn is honoured for nothing, while his younger brothers are passed over for the same nothing. Nature does not hand down wisdom or courage by the eldest son. Nor should honour be carried so, for it will be worn out in a few generations, and the badge will outlive the deed it commemorates.

I would rather the officers have their society and their badge for their own lives, and let it die with them. Honour paid to the father who earned it is just; I once heard it said that the Chinese give honours to a man's ancestors rather than his heirs, which at least rewards the one who is dead and cannot be spoiled by it. Let them keep their friendships, and give their charity to the poor among them. I am told that many members share these doubts, and I would hope they may amend the rules. And as for the bird chosen for the badge, I could say something there, but I will save it for a gayer letter.
