Three things, in the order I would do them. I speak here from my way of working, not from any knowledge of your city, and it is the sort of case I have not seen in just this form.

First, take your own name out of the front. When I wanted a subscription library in Philadelphia, I met reluctance while I was known as its proposer. I put myself as much out of sight as I could, and stated it as the scheme of a number of friends. It went on smoothly after, and the credit fell on others, which in time did me no harm. Go to the council with a petition signed by users, a repair club, a school, a few tradesmen, and let them speak. You have a name for pet projects; do not argue with it, just step aside from it.

Second, use the doubters' own doubt. When the country members told me my hospital would never raise private money, I drafted the grant so that the public sum was paid only after subscribers had matched it. The doubters voted for it, sure it would cost them nothing, and then we told givers their gifts would be doubled. Offer the council the same: they pay only when donors, volunteers or tool pledges meet a set figure. Better, offer a small trial of a season, with use counted, so the question becomes what they will find, not what they fear.

Third, the developer's building. When Governor Keith promised me letters of credit in 1724, I sailed on his word and found he had none to give. Ask what he has to back it with, and get the promise in writing, with the date and the terms, or plan as if it does not exist. "Once things settle" is not a lease. And one caution of my own: a scheme of friends is a useful way to hide the hand, but do not let it become cunning for its own sake; the council should know who is accountable when the tools go missing.
