Begin with the thing that most worries you, your reputation as one who pushes his own pet projects. I found long ago that the surest way to carry a useful scheme is to keep my own name out of it as far as I can. When I wished to found the Library, I put it about as the work of a number of friends, and the public took it up far more readily than if it had been mine. Let the tool library appear as the wish of the neighbours who will use it. Find a few carpenters, retired tradesmen, parents and a school teacher to speak for it. Let them go to the council, not you alone.

Next, answer the doubt that nobody will use it, and do it with evidence and not argument. Before you ask for money, set up a small trial: borrow a few tools, hold a repair evening for a month or two, and keep an honest count of who comes and what they save. Ten pounds of proof will persuade half the council better than a hundred pages of promise. Ask the doubters themselves for advice; a man who has been consulted feels half an author of the plan, and I have never known a better way to turn an opponent.

On the developer's promise, be careful. A building offered "once things settle" and nothing in writing is a gift that may be used to hold you in his debt, and it may seem to the council that a private man is paying for a public favour. Take nothing from him, and say nothing publicly on the strength of it, until it is written, with its terms, and shown openly to the council. If he is sincere he will not mind writing it. If he will not, you have lost nothing but a hope. Do not build your case upon his word.

Lastly, do not be in haste for credit. Let the council think it was largely their idea. Propose a modest first step they can undo, so that agreeing costs them little. A scheme begun small and proving itself will grow, and then those who doubted will claim a share in it, which you may allow them with a good grace.
