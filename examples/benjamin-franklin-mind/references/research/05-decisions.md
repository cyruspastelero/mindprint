# 05 · Decisions · Benjamin Franklin
Researched: 2026-11-28 · Sources: S501–S506, with the Autobiography [S101] and letters from tracks 02–04 · Agent: serial

Past-tense stories: year, situation and options, what he did, what followed, and his own account where one exists.

## Findings

### 1723 · Leaving his brother's printing house
- Did: When his brother James was barred from printing the Courant, the paper was put out under Benjamin's name. His old indenture was cancelled for show, and a secret new one was signed. After a fresh quarrel he claimed his freedom, betting James would not dare produce the secret papers. Then he left Boston [S101].
- Said: "It was not fair in me to take this advantage, and this I therefore reckon one of the first errata of my life" [S101].
- I infer: Fifty years on he records the unfair act and the provocation (James's blows) side by side. He makes no excuse and gives no full apology.

### 1724 · Governor Keith's empty promises
- Did: Sailed to London trusting Keith's promise of letters of credit. On arrival he found none. His friend Denham told him Keith had "no credit to give" [S101].
- Did: Followed Denham's advice and took work in London printing houses to improve his trade [S101].
- I infer: He learned early to check whether the person making a promise has the means to keep it.

### 1730 · Marriage
- Did: Took Deborah Read "to wife" in 1730 without a church ceremony. Her first husband had vanished but was not known to be dead, and his debts might have fallen on Franklin. Fisher calls it a common-law marriage [S403, S101].
- Did: William, born to another woman around this time, was raised in the household [S403].

### 1731 · The library subscription
- Did: Found that people resisted "the proposer of any useful project". So he put himself "as much as I could out of sight" and presented the library as "a scheme of a number of friends" [S101].
- Did: Used the same approach for later projects. The hospital, he notes, was Dr. Bond's idea, "ascrib'd to me, but was originally his" [S101].

### 1735 · The Hemphill affair
- Did: Backed a young preacher whose sermons stressed good works over doctrine. When Hemphill was found to be preaching other men's sermons, most of his party left him. Franklin stayed, saying he "rather approv'd his giving us good sermons compos'd by others, than bad ones of his own manufacture" [S101].
- Did: After the synod's ruling, he left the congregation for good but kept paying his subscription to support its ministers [S101].

### 1736 · His son Francis and smallpox
- Did: Did not have his four-year-old son inoculated. The boy died of smallpox "taken in the common way" [S101].
- Said: "I long regretted bitterly, and still regret that I had not given it to him by inoculation." [S101]
- Said: He turned it into advice for other parents. Since "the regret may be the same either way", "the safer should be chosen" [S101].

### 1739 · Whitefield's collection
- Did: Disagreed with Whitefield's plan to build an orphan house in Georgia and "silently resolved he should get nothing from me". Then, during the sermon, he gave the coppers, then the silver, and finally "empty'd my pocket wholly into the collector's dish, gold and all" [S101].
- I infer: He tells this against himself, as evidence of how oratory beats a resolution.

### 1742 · The stove and the patent
- Did: Turned down Governor Thomas's offer of a patent for the Pennsylvania fireplace, "That, as we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours" [S101].
- Did: Did not sue the London ironmonger who patented a worsened copy [S101].

### 1747–48 · Retiring from business; the Quaker assembly and war money
- Did: Took David Hall as partner; Hall "took off my hands all care of the printing-office", paying him his share for eighteen years [S101].
- Did: Recorded with approval how the Quaker-run Assembly, unable in conscience to vote money for gunpowder, voted it for "bread, flour, wheat, or other grain", and the Governor read "other grain" as gunpowder [S101].

### 1751 · The hospital and the matching clause
- Did: Faced an Assembly sure the hospital could never raise private money. He drafted the bill with a conditional clause: public money would come only once subscribers had raised a matching sum. The members passed it, thinking it would cost nothing. That condition then helped raise the subscriptions [S101].

### 1755 · Braddock's wagons
- Did: Raised 150 wagons from Pennsylvania farmers for General Braddock, and gave his personal bond when the owners would not trust the General's promise [S101].
- Said: Warned Braddock of "ambuscades of Indians" against a column "near four miles long". Braddock "smil'd at my ignorance" [S101].
- Outcome: After the defeat, owners sued Franklin for claims of "near twenty thousand pound, which to pay would have ruined me", until General Shirley had the claims paid [S101].

### 1764 · The Paxton march
- Did: Supported a Proprietary governor he disliked, wrote the Narrative against the murderers of the Conestoga, raised armed volunteers, and served as a common soldier and envoy to the marchers [S210, S309].

### 1765–1766 · Stamp Act: wrong, then right
- Did: After the Act passed he advised making the best of it: "We may still light candles". He named his friend John Hughes as stamp distributor and sent stamped paper home to be sold [S506, S403].
- Said: To Hughes: "A firm loyalty to the crown ... will always be the wisest course for you and I to take." [S506]
- Outcome: Mobs threatened Hughes's house and Franklin's. Fisher records that Deborah refused to leave [S403]. Franklin "change[d] his ground" (Fisher's words) and in February 1766 gave the testimony that helped bring repeal [S403, S201].

### 1775 · Strahan
- Did: Wrote "You are now my Enemy" to his oldest London friend, an MP who had voted for the war, and did not send it [S306].

### 1776 · Lord Howe's peace offer
- Did: Refused Howe's offer of pardons in writing. A pardon offered to "the very Parties injured" could only increase resentment. He left open a treaty "as distinct States" and doubted Howe had powers for one [S503].
- Said: "Long did I endeavour, with unfeigned and unwearied Zeal, to preserve from breaking that fine and noble China Vase, the British Empire" [S503].
- Said: In the same letter he accuses Britain of having excited "our Slaves to murder their Masters" [S503].

### 1784 · Mesmer's animal magnetism
- Did: Sat on the royal commission. Some trials were held at his house in Passy. The commissioners blindfolded subjects and pretended to magnetise them. The effects followed what subjects believed was happening, not what was done, and the commission concluded imagination was the cause [S502].

### 1784–1788 · William
- Did: Answered William's overture in 1784. He was glad to revive their intercourse, but "nothing has ever hurt me so much and affected me with such keen Sensations, as to find myself deserted in my old Age by my only Son" [S504].
- Said: In the same letter he concedes: "I ought not to blame you for differing in Sentiment with me in Public Affairs. We are Men, all subject to Errors." [S504]
- Did: Left William little in the will, which explains: "The part he acted against me in the late war, which is of public notoriety ... will account for my leaving him no more of an estate he endeavoured to deprive me of." [S505]

### 1750 → 1790 · Slavery
- Did: Wrote to his mother in 1750 that he planned to sell an enslaved couple [S404].
- Did: Kept enslaved servants with him in London (Peter, King) [S404]. Waldstreicher puts his ownership at about 1735–1781 [S406].
- Did: As President, signed the Pennsylvania Abolition Society's 1789 address and its memorial to the first Congress [S501, S305].
- Said: The address: "Slavery is such an atrocious debasement of human nature, that its very extirpation, if not performed with solicitous care, may sometimes open a source of serious evils." [S501]
- Did: According to Waldstreicher, he declined to raise slavery at the 1787 Convention when the Society asked him to [S406].

## Contradictions
- The patent refusal ("serve others by any invention") [S101] vs eagerly milking the French court for money (Lawrence's words) [S402]. Both are service to a cause. The critic and the subject read the same act differently.
- He would not judge William's politics in 1784 ("Our Opinions are not in our own Power") [S504], and yet the will punished William for those politics [S505].
- The abolition address [S501] vs staying silent on slavery at the Convention [S406] and owning slaves into the 1780s [S406].

## Differs from prior
- [overturn] Prior: civic projects as Franklin's own creations. He hid his authorship on purpose, and the hospital was Bond's idea [S101].
- [new] The hospital matching clause: he used the opponents' disbelief that private money would come as the lever to pass the bill [S101].
- [new] He kept a preacher he knew was reciting other men's sermons, because the content was better [S101].
- [deepen] Prior: regret is not part of the folk image. His regret over not inoculating Francis became his argument for choosing the safer risk [S101].
- [overturn] Prior: steady patriot. In 1765 he urged loyalty and named a friend as stamp officer, then reversed under pressure [S506, S403].
- [new] Blind trials at his house in Passy to test a fashionable cure [S502].
- [deepen] Prior: unsure of the end with William. He wrote that nothing had hurt him so much, conceded that opinions are not in our power, and still cut William out of the will [S504, S505].
