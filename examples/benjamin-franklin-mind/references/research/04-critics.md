# 04 · Critics · Benjamin Franklin
Researched: 2026-11-28 · Sources: S401–S406 · Agent: serial

Two public-domain literary attacks (Twain, Lawrence), two older debunking or close-reading biographies (Fisher, Bruce), one contemporary defence that records a contemporary accusation (Priestley answering Pennant), and one modern historian's essay on slavery (Waldstreicher, not archivable; no quotes taken from it).

## Findings

### 1870 · Mark Twain, "The Late Benjamin Franklin"
- Others said: Twain attacks the use of Franklin as a model for boys, not Franklin's public service: his maxims were "calculated to inflict suffering upon the rising generation of all subsequent ages" [S401].
- Others said: The aphorisms were "worked up with a great show of originality out of truisms that had become wearisome platitudes as early as the dispersion from Babel" [S401].
- Others said: His real point: the habits were "only the evidences of genius, not the creators of it" [S401].
- I infer: Twain's target is the Franklin of Poor Richard, the one later parents used. Franklin himself had already written that Poor Richard's wisdom was gleaned and that the crowd ignored it [S102].

### 1923 · D. H. Lawrence, Studies in Classic American Literature
- Others said: Franklin "set up the first dummy American" [S402].
- Others said: Of the list of virtues: "He made himself a list of virtues, which he trotted inside like a grey nag in a paddock." [S402]
- Others said: Lawrence quotes the Autobiography's rum passage about the Indians and answers it in anger [S402, S101].
- Others said: He admits admiration for Franklin's courage and still concludes "I do not like him." [S402]
- Others said: On Paris: Franklin was delighted "when he was at Paris judiciously milking money out of the French monarchy for the overthrow of all monarchy" [S402].
- I infer: Lawrence's charge is the most serious one for this skill. Franklin reduces the self to what is useful socially, and something real gets cut off. The skill should feel that pressure, not wave it away.

### 1898 · Sydney George Fisher, The True Benjamin Franklin
- Others said: Fisher wrote against the expurgated Franklin of nineteenth-century biography. He says many books describe "the Franklin that never existed" [S403].
- Others said: On the marriage: there is no record of a ceremony; Fisher treats it as a common-law marriage they both regarded as binding [S403].
- Others said: William was born to another woman shortly after the 1730 union. Fisher says there is no evidence Deborah was his mother. He cites the diary of Daniel Fisher, who lived in the house as his clerk, for Deborah calling the grown William "the greatest Villain upon Earth" [S403]. This is one witness's account of a household quarrel. Treat it as testimony, not established fact.
- Others said: Fisher also reports a claim, from one manuscript letter, of a second illegitimate child, a daughter. He admits it was disputed and argues it in an appendix [S403]. Contested; left out of the skill.
- Others said: On the Stamp Act, Franklin misjudged colonial anger in 1765. He nominated his friend John Hughes as stamp distributor and sent stamped paper to his partner to sell. He changed ground only after the riots [S403, S506].
- Others said: The Pennsylvania Gazette carried the same ads as other papers, including "runaway servants and slaves" [S403].

### 1917 · William Cabell Bruce, Benjamin Franklin Self-Revealed
- Others said: Bruce, writing from the letters, sets out the break with William. William kept his royal office, was arrested as a Tory, and led the Associated Loyalists. Father and son met once, briefly, at Southampton as Franklin sailed home in 1785, and were never reconciled [S404].
- Others said: Bruce prints the 1784 letter and the will clause, both archived separately as their own sources [S504, S505].
- Others said: Bruce reports that Franklin's letters to Deborah from London mention Peter, an enslaved servant, and King, who ran away in England and was taken in by a lady who educated him [S404].
- Others said: In a letter to his mother (1750), Franklin wrote that they meant to sell an enslaved couple "for we do not like Negro Servants". Bruce notes the irony against his later presidency of the Abolition Society [S404].
- Others said: William wrote to his father after Deborah's death in 1774 that she had not expected to see him again unless he came home that winter, and that the disappointment "preyed a good deal on her spirits" [S404].

### 1802 · Priestley answering Pennant
- Others said: Thomas Pennant had written that Franklin, living under British protection, "was secretly playing the incendiary" in America. Priestley answers from his own conversations with Franklin before the war [S405].

### 2004 · David Waldstreicher, _Benjamin Franklin, Slavery, and the Founders_ (Common-place 4.4)
- Others said: Waldstreicher argues that Franklin owned a series of slaves from about 1735 to 1781 and never systematically freed them. He did not so much change his mind as manage the blame. As president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society he declined the Society's request to raise slavery at the 1787 Convention [S406]. Not archived: paraphrase only.

## Contradictions
- Waldstreicher: no real conversion, only deflection [S406]. Against this: in his last years Franklin signed the Society's address calling slavery "an atrocious debasement of human nature" [S501] and attacked the trade in print weeks before his death [S305]. Both stand.
- Lawrence: a man with no inner depths [S402]. Against this: Franklin's own record of regret over Francis's death and his self-mockery [S101]. The skill should hold both.

## Gaps
- No modern scholarly biography could be archived (Lemay, Wood, Isaacson, Skemp on William). Their findings are not used.
- John Adams's hostile letters about Franklin in Paris were not archived. His criticism appears only through Lawrence's echo of it.

## Differs from prior
- [overturn] Prior: 'slavery views turned late, owned some slaves'. The record puts it more sharply: enslaved people in his household for decades, including in London [S404]; a plan to sell a couple in 1750 because "we do not like Negro Servants" [S404]; and according to Waldstreicher, ownership into the 1780s [S406].
- [new] His most quoted critics attack the Poor Richard image more than the man. Twain says the habits did not make the genius [S401]; Lawrence says the system shrinks the soul [S402].
- [new] On the Stamp Act he misjudged colonial anger, nominated a friend as stamp officer, and changed course only after the riots [S403, S506].
- [deepen] Prior: unsure whether he reconciled with William. One brief meeting in 1785, no reconciliation, and a will that names "the part he acted against me in the late war" [S404, S505].
- [new] Deborah died in 1774 while he stayed in London. Her son's letter records her disappointment that he did not come home [S404].
