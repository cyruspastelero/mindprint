# 01 · Writings · Benjamin Franklin
Researched: 2026-11-28 · Sources: S101–S110 · Agent: serial (one agent, tracks run in order)

All texts are Project Gutenberg editions, archived in `references/sources/` with the Gutenberg header and licence removed. Most short pieces come from Mott & Jorgenson's 1936 selection (Gutenberg #35508), which prints Franklin's own spelling and capitals.

## Findings

### 1771–1790 · The Autobiography as a working manual, not a confession
- Said: He calls his mistakes "errata", printer's corrections, and lists them as things he tried to fix later: breaking faith with Deborah Read, spending Vernon's money, the 1725 deist pamphlet [S101].
- Said: The vegetarian episode ends with a joke against his own reasoning: "So convenient a thing it is to be a reasonable creature, since it enables one to find or make a reason for everything one has a mind to do." [S101]
- Said: On humility he admits he got the look of it, not the thing: "I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the reality of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the appearance of it." [S101]
- Did: He dropped words that "imported a fix'd opinion, such as certainly, undoubtedly" and used "I conceive, I apprehend, or I imagine" instead [S101].
- Said: The speckled-axe story: he admits that something "that pretended to be reason" kept suggesting his moral precision was "a kind of foppery in morals" [S101].
- I infer: The book is written to be used. Each episode ends with a rule the reader can take away. The self-mockery is part of the method: he shows the rule working on himself, flaws included.

### 1725 → 1771 · Liberty and Necessity, and its recantation
- Did: At 19 in London he printed a pamphlet arguing that, God being all-wise and all-powerful, vice and virtue were "empty distinctions" [S103, S101].
- Said: Later he judged the doctrine by its effects on people he knew (Collins, Ralph, Keith, his own conduct): "I began to suspect that this doctrine, tho' it might be true, was not very useful." [S101]
- I infer: He rejects a belief on the grounds that it does not work, while leaving open that it might be true. He uses that test again and again.

### 1728 · Articles of Belief and Acts of Religion
- Did: Wrote his own private liturgy with a creed, prayers and a hymn, for his own use [S104].
- Others said: Lawrence later mocks the public creed drawn from these lines as made to "satisfy the professors of every religion, but shock none" [S402].

### 1751 · Observations Concerning the Increase of Mankind
- Did: Worked out from colonial data that Americans doubled about every 20–25 years, and that cheap land, not policy, drove early marriage and growth [S105].
- Said: The same essay ends with a nativist and racial passage: he asks why "the Palatine Boors" should "swarm into our Settlements", and why "increase the Sons of Africa, by planting them in America" [S105].
- Did: According to the editors' note, he cut those concluding paragraphs in later editions after John Bartram and Cadwallader Colden objected [S105].

### 1731 · An Apology for Printers
- Said: A printer's trade is to serve all sides, on the belief that "when Truth and Error have fair Play, the former is always an overmatch for the latter" [S109].
- Did: The same piece explains he still refused some paid work he judged would do harm [S109].

### 1758 · The Way to Wealth
- Did: Wrote it as a frame story. Father Abraham strings together Poor Richard's sayings at an auction, and the crowd "approved the doctrine, and immediately practised the contrary, just as if it had been a common sermon" [S102].
- Said: Richard admits the wisdom was not his own: "not a tenth part of the wisdom was my own" [S102].
- I infer: The famous thrift text is a satire of preaching thrift. Its narrator is a persona looking at a second persona.

### 1773 · Rules by Which a Great Empire May Be Reduced to a Small One
- Did: Wrote twenty ironic rules, each describing a real British measure as advice for losing colonies [S110].
- Said (in a letter to William): he chose "out-of-the-way forms, as most likely to take the general attention" [S209].

### 1782–1784 · Information to Those Who Would Remove to America; Remarks Concerning the Savages
- Said: America asks of a stranger "What is he? but, What can he do?" [S106]. Birth "is a Commodity that cannot be carried to a worse Market than that of America" [S106].
- Said: "Savages we call them, because their Manners differ from ours, which we think the Perfection of Civility; they think the same of theirs." [S107]
- Did: The Remarks argue by a string of anecdotes, each showing Native politeness or reasoning that shames colonial manners [S107].

### 1784 · Letter to Benjamin Vaughan on luxury
- Said: Asked for a remedy for American luxury, he answered that he had none and doubted it needed one: "Is not the Hope of one day being able to purchase and enjoy Luxuries a great Spur to Labour and Industry?" [S108]
- Did: Backed the point with a story: a cap his wife gave to a skipper's daughter led the girls of Cape May to knit mittens for sale [S108].

## Contradictions
- He sees through his own rationalising (the cod) [S101], and he also builds a whole ethics on whether a belief is useful [S101]. The same habit of mind both protects him and gives him an excuse.
- He mocks prejudice about "savages" and says birth counts for nothing in America [S106, S107], and in 1751 he wrote the racial passage about "purely white People" [S105].
- Thrift preacher [S102] vs defender of luxury as a spur to industry [S108].

## Gaps
- Scientific papers (Experiments and Observations on Electricity) were not archived. The electrical work enters only through the Autobiography.
- Pennsylvania Gazette editorial copy before 1748 was not archived beyond the Apology.
- The 1735 Gazette letter on fire prevention, the usual source of "an ounce of prevention", could not be archived.

## Differs from prior
- [overturn] Prior: Poor Richard is Franklin's sincere voice of thrift. The record: The Way to Wealth is a framed satire whose audience ignores the sermon, and Richard says most of the wisdom was not his [S102].
- [overturn] Prior: thrift as a moral absolute. Asked about luxury in 1784, he defended it as a spur to labour [S108].
- [deepen] Prior: "self-improvement by system". The record: the system came with self-mockery. He admits he got the "appearance" of humility, not the reality, and he knows reason can be made to justify appetite [S101].
- [new] He judges ideas by their effects on people ("not very useful"), not only by their truth, starting with his own 1725 pamphlet [S101, S103].
- [new] The 1751 essay's racial and anti-German passage, cut later after friends objected [S105].
