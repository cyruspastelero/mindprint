# 02 · Conversations · Benjamin Franklin
Researched: 2026-11-28 · Sources: S201–S210 · Agent: serial

No recordings or interviews exist. The nearest things to unscripted speech are his testimony under questioning in the House of Commons, his floor speeches at the 1787 Convention, and letters that answer a direct question from a friend. All are archived.

## Findings

### 1766 · Examination before the House of Commons
- Did: Answered roughly 170 questions over the Stamp Act, many hostile [S201].
- Said: Asked what the temper of America toward Britain had been before 1763: "The best in the world." [S201]
- Said: Asked whether anything less than a military force could carry the act into execution, he turned the question round: "Suppose a military force sent into America, they will find nobody in arms; what are they then to do?" [S201]
- Said: His closing exchange: what used to be the pride of Americans was "To indulge in the fashions and manufactures of Great Britain"; now it is "To wear their old clothes over again, till they can make new ones." [S201]
- I infer: Under hostile questioning he answers short, concrete and in terms of what people will actually do (buy or not buy, comply or not). He argues about the practical consequence, not about rights in the abstract.

### 1772 · Letter to Priestley: "how, not what"
- Said: "I cannot for want of sufficient Premises, advise you what to determine, but if you please I will tell you how." [S202]
- Did: Described his method: pro and con columns kept over "three or four Days", striking out reasons of equal weight against each other, then waiting "a Day or two of farther Consideration" before deciding [S202].
- Said: He admits the weights "cannot be taken with the Precision of Algebraic Quantities", and still finds the method makes him "less liable to make a rash Step" [S202].
- I infer: When a friend brings him a personal decision, he will not give an opinion on the facts. He hands over a procedure. The timing matters in the method: he collects reasons over days, because different reasons come to mind at different times.

### 1773 · Letter to William Franklin on politics between them
- Said: "You are a thorough government man, which I do not wonder at, nor do I aim at converting you. I only wish you to act uprightly and steadily, avoiding that duplicity, which in Hutchinson, adds contempt to indignation." [S209]
- Said: He tells his son that a published piece was reprinted "stripped of all the capitaling and italicing, that intimate the allusions and mark the emphasis" [S209]. He cared about typography as part of how an argument persuades.

### 1764 · Letter to Fothergill on the Paxton march
- Said: In a few weeks he had been "a common soldier, a counsellor, a kind of dictator, an ambassador to the country mob, and on his returning home, nobody again" [S210].
- Did: Though opposed to the Proprietor, he "considered government as government", wrote a pamphlet against the rioters, organised armed volunteers, and refused command so as to carry a musket [S210].

### 1780 · Dialogue between Franklin and the Gout
- Did: Wrote a mock quarrel in which the Gout scolds him for chess, carriages and idleness, and he loses every exchange [S203].
- I infer: When he wants to admit a fault, he often stages the reproach as a dialogue in which he plays the loser.

### 1784 · Letter to Samuel Mather
- Said: A book by Cotton Mather gave him a view that shaped his life: "I have always set a greater value on the character of a doer of good, than on any other kind of reputation" [S204].
- Said: Cotton Mather's warning to him in 1724: "STOOP as you go through it, and you will miss many hard thumps" [S204].

### 1787 · Convention speeches
- Said: Final speech: "I confess, that I do not entirely approve of this Constitution at present; but, Sir, I am not sure I shall never approve it" [S205].
- Said: "the older I grow, the more apt I am to doubt my own judgment of others." [S205]
- Said: "when you assemble a number of men, to have the advantage of their joint wisdom, you inevitably assemble with those men all their prejudices, their passions, their errors of opinion, their local interests, and their selfish views." [S205]
- Said: He promised to keep his objections inside the room: "Within these walls they were born, and here they shall die." [S205]
- Said (to Dupont de Nemours, 1788, quoted in the editors' note): "The wisest must agree to some unreasonable things, that reasonable ones of more consequence may be obtained." [S205]
- Did: Earlier in the summer he moved an amendment of his own on the executive. Details are sealed in holdout.md [S206].

### 1784, 1790 · Letters answering direct questions
- Two more letters answer a question put to him directly, one from his daughter and one from a college president. Both are sealed as holdout items and kept out of extraction [S207, S208].

## Contradictions
- He promised to keep his objections to the Constitution inside the room [S205]. The same speech, which talks about those objections, was then circulated. I could not archive a primary record of who circulated it, so this is left as an open question, not a finding.
- He will not tell Priestley what to decide [S202], yet he lectures his son on how to be a governor [S209].

## Gaps
- No transcript of his spoken words survives beyond formal settings. Table talk reaches us only through others (Jefferson mentions his "bon mots" but records none [S601]).
- The Hutchinson letters affair (1772–74) and the Privy Council hearing of January 1774 were not archived. They belong in a future update.

## Differs from prior
- [deepen] Prior: "diplomacy by charm". Under hostile questioning in 1766 his method was short answers framed as what people will actually do, ending on a line about homespun clothes [S201].
- [new] He declined to give friends a verdict on their own decisions and gave them a procedure instead, with the wait built in [S202].
- [deepen] Prior: "signed the Constitution despite doubts". The record: he made doubting your own infallibility the argument for signing, and he named assemblies' passions and selfish views as the reason no better document was coming [S205].
- [new] Toward his son he separated the political view from the conduct: no attempt to convert him, but a demand that he act "uprightly and steadily" [S209].
