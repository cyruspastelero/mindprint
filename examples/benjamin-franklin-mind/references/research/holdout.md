# Holdout · Benjamin Franklin
Sealed: 2026-11-28. Test items only. Nothing here may appear in SKILL.md, casebook.md or voice.md.

Chosen as small, specific judgements from primary texts, not famous events. Each one can be predicted from how he reasons, but not from the popular image.

## H1 · Paying the chief executive, 1787
Source: [S206]
Question: It is June 1787 and you are a delegate at the Federal Convention in Philadelphia. The draft plan gives the national executive a fixed salary. Do you support paying the executive, and why or why not?
What happened: Franklin rose to oppose any salary for the executive and moved an amendment against it. His reasoning was that ambition and avarice, joined in one post of honour and profit, draw the bold and violent rather than the wise, breed faction, and push salaries ever upward until a monarchy grows out of it. He offered evidence that unpaid service works: English high sheriffs, Quaker meetings settling disputes, and Washington commanding eight years without pay. He said that if the motion were not seconded or accepted he would be content with having given his opinion frankly and done his duty.

## H2 · An hereditary order of veterans, 1784
Source: [S207]
Question: It is 1784 and you are abroad. Your daughter sends newspapers about a new society of the Revolution's officers, whose membership and badge are to pass down to their eldest sons. What is your view of it?
What happened: In a letter to his daughter he called a hereditary order a direct contradiction of the country's declared dislike of nobility, though he would not mind the officers wearing ribbons themselves. He argued that honour fairly earned is personal and should, as among the Chinese, ascend to the parents who raised the man, not descend to children who did nothing for it. He made the case arithmetically: after nine generations the founder's share in his heir is one part in 512, while the heir has 1,022 ancestors in those generations. Descending honour, he said, makes heirs proud and idle.

## H3 · Questioned on his religion at eighty-four
Source: [S208]
Question: You are eighty-four and frail. An old friend who heads a college writes asking, for the first time, what your religion is, and especially what you think of Jesus of Nazareth. How do you answer, and do you attach any condition?
What happened: He answered with a short creed: one God who made and governs the world, who should be worshipped, whose most acceptable service is doing good to his other children, and an immortal soul judged justly. On Jesus he praised the morals as the best the world had seen but said the religion had been corrupted, and admitted doubts about the divinity without dogmatising, remarking that he would soon find out with less trouble. He saw no harm in others believing it if the belief made the teachings better observed. He asked the friend not to publish the letter, noting that he had always helped every sect build its meeting houses and wished to die at peace with all of them.

## H4 · A peace commissioner's offer of pardon, 1776
Source: [S503]
Question: It is July 1776. An English admiral you count as a personal friend writes that he has come with powers to restore peace, and encloses offers of pardon to colonists who submit. How do you reply?
What happened: He replied that offering pardons to the injured party only showed contempt and would deepen resentment, listing burned towns and hired mercenaries as wrongs that ended any affection for Britain. He allowed that a treaty between Britain and America as separate states might still be possible before foreign alliances, but said he was sure the admiral had no such powers. He recalled his own long effort to keep the empire whole and his tears when reconciliation once seemed near, called the war unjust and unwise, said trade is never worth bloodshed, and urged his friend to give up so odious a command.
