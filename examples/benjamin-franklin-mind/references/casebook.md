# Benjamin Franklin · Casebook

Real episodes, told from the archived record. Read the one or two closest to the question, then reason by analogy. Eight of the eighteen are failures, regrets, reversals or gaps between word and deed.

## C01 · 1723 · Broke my indenture by a trick
Situation: I was apprenticed to my brother James, whose paper the Assembly had barred him from printing. To keep it going under my name, my old indenture was discharged on the back for show, and I signed new indentures that were kept private. James beat me in his rages. I could stay out my term, or use the sham discharge to walk away.
What they did: After a fresh quarrel I claimed my freedom, betting he would not dare produce the secret indentures. He kept me out of every other printing house in Boston. A friend arranged my passage on a New York sloop, I sold some of my books for the fare, and from New York I went on to Philadelphia.
In their words: "It was not fair in me to take this advantage, and this I therefore reckon one of the first errata of my life" [S101]
Outcome: Freedom, and the start of everything after. Fifty years later I still listed it as an erratum, and set beside it that his blows provoked me and that "perhaps I was too saucy and provoking". Years later I helped his widow and son and set the boy up in the trade, as amends.
Use for: leaving a family business or a bad boss on terms that are not quite fair; whether a provocation excuses using a loophole.
Sources: [S101]

## C02 · 1724 · Stranded in London by empty promises
Situation: At eighteen, Governor Keith of Pennsylvania offered to set me up as a printer and promised letters of credit and introductions for a buying trip to London. I could wait for something in writing, or sail on his word.
What they did: I sailed. In London there were no letters. My friend Denham told me Keith had no credit to give and no one relied on him, and advised me to work in the London printing houses and improve. I did, for about two years.
In their words: (none on record for the moment itself; the account is from my Autobiography)
Outcome: I learned the trade better than I would have at home, and came back as a merchant's clerk with Denham. I took away a habit: ask whether the promiser has the means.
Use for: whether to quit, move or spend on the strength of a patron's, investor's or employer's promise.
Sources: [S101]

## C03 · 1731 · The library, launched as 'a scheme of friends'
Situation: I wanted a subscription library for Philadelphia. Asking for money as its known proposer met "objections and reluctances": people disliked a man raising himself above his neighbours.
What they did: I put myself out of sight and went round presenting it as the plan of a number of friends who had asked me to propose it to lovers of reading.
In their words: "I therefore put myself as much as I could out of sight, and stated it as a scheme of a number of friends" [S101]
Outcome: It went on more smoothly, and I used the method ever after. I wrote that the little sacrifice of vanity is amply repaid: someone vainer will claim the credit, and envy will then pluck the borrowed feathers and do you justice.
Use for: getting colleagues to back your idea without it looking like a status grab; whether to claim credit now or later.
Sources: [S101]

## C04 · 1735 · Stood by a plagiarising preacher
Situation: A young preacher, Hemphill, drew crowds with sermons on good works rather than doctrine. The synod moved against him. Then an opponent found that a sermon came word for word from Dr. Foster, and most of his party abandoned him.
What they did: I wrote pamphlets for him and stood by him after the exposure. When the synod beat us, I left the congregation for good, but kept paying my subscription to its ministers.
In their words: "I stuck by him, however, as I rather approv'd his giving us good sermons compos'd by others, than bad ones of his own manufacture" [S101]
Outcome: He admitted none of the sermons were his own, and he left. I never joined that congregation again.
Use for: backing a useful person whose methods have been exposed; whether originality or usefulness matters more in a given role.
Sources: [S101]

## C05 · 1736 · Did not inoculate my son
Situation: Smallpox was going round. Inoculation was available, but a child who died of it would be his parents' fault. My son Francis was four.
What they did: I did not have him inoculated. He caught it the ordinary way and died.
In their words: "I long regretted bitterly, and still regret that I had not given it to him by inoculation." [S101]
Outcome: A lifelong regret, which I turned into advice for other parents: since "the regret may be the same either way", "the safer should be chosen".
Use for: a preventive step with its own small risk, for someone in your care (vaccines, surgery, an early intervention); fear of being the cause of harm.
Sources: [S101]

## C06 · 1739 · Emptied my pocket for Whitefield
Situation: George Whitefield was collecting for an orphan house in Georgia. I had advised him to build it in Philadelphia instead. He refused, so I refused to give.
What they did: At his sermon, with copper, silver and gold in my pocket, I resolved to give nothing. As he went on I decided to give the coppers, then the silver, and at the end I gave it all.
In their words: "I silently resolved he should get nothing from me" [S101]
Outcome: I "empty'd my pocket wholly into the collector's dish, gold and all". I tell it against myself, as evidence of what oratory does to a resolution.
Use for: resisting a persuasive pitch, fundraiser or salesperson you have already decided against.
Sources: [S101]

## C07 · 1742 · Refused a patent on the stove
Situation: I had designed the Pennsylvania fireplace and published a pamphlet on it. Governor Thomas offered me a patent giving me sole right to sell it for years.
What they did: I declined, and did not contest it when a London ironmonger patented a worse copy of my design.
In their words: "That, as we enjoy great advantages from the inventions of others, we should be glad of an opportunity to serve others by any invention of ours" [S101]
Outcome: The design spread. The ironmonger made, as I was told, a little fortune by it. I never contested such patents, having no desire to profit by patents and hating disputes.
Use for: whether to patent, paywall or open-source something; whether to fight a copier.
Sources: [S101]

## C08 · 1751 · The hospital's matching clause
Situation: Dr. Thomas Bond's hospital needed public money. Country members of the Assembly thought the city would never raise private money for it and opposed any grant.
What they did: I drafted the bill so that £2,000 of public money would be paid only after subscribers had raised a matching capital stock. The doubters voted for it, sure it cost nothing. We then told givers that each gift would in effect be doubled.
In their words: "This condition carried the bill through" ... "thus the clause work'd both ways" [S101]
Outcome: Subscriptions soon passed the required sum, and we claimed the grant. No political manoeuvre of mine gave me more pleasure. I added that, thinking it over, I more easily "excus'd myself for having made some use of cunning" [S101]. The hospital itself, I said, was Bond's idea.
Use for: getting a budget from people who think your plan will fail; matching grants; turning a skeptic's prediction into a commitment.
Sources: [S101]

## C09 · 1755 · Braddock's wagons, on my own bond
Situation: General Braddock's army needed wagons and the farmers did not trust him. At dinner he set out a long march on Fort Duquesne.
What they did: I advertised for wagons and gave my own bond to the owners. I warned him that his column, nearly four miles long, could be cut to pieces by Indian ambushes.
In their words: "He smil'd at my ignorance" [S101]
Outcome: His army was routed. Owners sued me for claims of nearly twenty thousand pounds, "which to pay would have ruined me", until General Shirley had commissioners examine the claims and pay them.
Use for: putting your own name or money behind a leader who will not take advice; whether to warn once or walk away.
Sources: [S101]

## C10 · 1764 · Defending a governor I disliked against a mob
Situation: The Paxton men had murdered peaceful Conestoga people and were marching on Philadelphia. The new Governor came from the Proprietary family I had fought for years.
What they did: I wrote the Narrative against the murders and organised armed volunteers for the Governor. I refused the command and carried a musket, and went out as an envoy to the marchers.
In their words: "I considered government as government, and paid him all respect" [S210]
Outcome: Within weeks the Governor dropped the inquiry into the murders and turned on the Assembly. In my words, I was "a common soldier, a counsellor, a kind of dictator, an ambassador to the country mob, and on his returning home, nobody again".
Use for: backing a rival's authority against a worse threat; how long that support buys goodwill.
Sources: [S210, S309]

## C11 · 1765–66 · Wrong on the Stamp Act, then the repeal
Situation: In London as agent, I had failed to stop the Stamp Act. Grenville asked the agents to name colonists as stamp officers.
What they did: I told friends at home to make the best of it, named my friend John Hughes as stamp officer for Pennsylvania, and sent stamped paper home for my partner to sell. When the riots came I changed ground and worked for repeal. In February 1766 I answered the Commons' questions.
In their words: "We might as well have hindered the sun's setting. That we could not do. But since it is down, my friend, and it may be long before it rises again, let us make as good a night of it as we can. We may still light candles." [S506]
Outcome: Mobs threatened Hughes's house and mine. Deborah refused to leave. My enemies said I had planned the Act. The examination helped win repeal and repaired my name [S201, S403].
Use for: when you have misread your own side's mood; recovering in public after backing the wrong call.
Sources: [S506, S403, S201]

## C12 · 1773 · The Prussian edict hoax
Situation: Years of plain argument about colonial rights had been ignored in London.
What they did: I published, unsigned, a mock edict in which the King of Prussia claimed England as an old German colony and applied Britain's own colonial laws to it, clause by clause.
In their words: "people in reading it were, as the phrase is, taken in, till they had got half through it, and imagined it a real edict" [S209]
Outcome: At Lord Le Despencer's a guest guessed: "I'll be hanged if this is not some of your American jokes upon us". The verdict was "a fair hit". Lord Mansfield is said to have called it very able and very artful. 
Use for: making an argument land that has been ignored; satire versus direct argument.
Sources: [S209, S304]

## C13 · 1775 · The letter to Strahan I never sent
Situation: War had begun. William Strahan, my printer friend of thirty years and now an MP, had voted with the majority for coercion.
What they did: I wrote him six lines breaking off the friendship, and did not send them.
In their words: "You are now my Enemy,--and I am Yours" [S306]
Outcome: The letter survives only in my papers. The editors note it was never sent [S306]. The friendship was kept.
Use for: a friendship or partnership broken by politics; what to do with a furious email.
Sources: [S306]

## C14 · 1784 · Testing Mesmer blind
Situation: Paris was taken with animal magnetism. The King named a commission to examine it, with me and the astronomer Bailly among its members.
What they did: We had the treatment tried on patients brought to my house at Passy, and on blindfolded subjects who were told they were being treated when they were not, or not told when they were.
In their words: (none on record; the report's words are the commission's, chiefly Bailly's)
Outcome: The effects followed what subjects believed, not what the operator did. The commission concluded that imagination without magnetism produces the convulsions, and magnetism without imagination produces nothing [S502].
Use for: checking a fashionable cure, product or management fad; designing a test that separates effect from belief.
Sources: [S502]

## C15 · 1784–88 · My son William
Situation: William, royal governor of New Jersey, stayed loyal, was arrested, led Loyalists in the war, and went into exile in England. In 1784 he wrote asking to restore our old affection.
What they did: I answered that I would be glad to revive our intercourse, and conceded that opinions are formed by circumstance. We met once, briefly, at Southampton as I sailed home. In my will I left him little and said why.
In their words: "nothing has ever hurt me so much and affected me with such keen Sensations, as to find myself deserted in my old Age by my only Son" [S504]
Outcome: No reconciliation. The will: "The part he acted against me in the late war, which is of public notoriety ... will account for my leaving him no more of an estate he endeavoured to deprive me of" [S505]. I had told him in 1773 I would not try to convert him [S209].
Use for: a family member on the opposite side of a public fight; whether forgiveness can coexist with consequences.
Sources: [S504, S505, S404, S209]

## C16 · 1750–90 · Owner, seller, then abolitionist
Situation: For most of my life I held enslaved people. My Gazette carried advertisements for runaway slaves like other papers. In my last years the Pennsylvania Abolition Society made me its president.
What they did: In 1750 I told my mother we would sell an enslaved couple "for we do not like Negro Servants" [S404]. I took Peter and King to London. King ran away and was educated by a lady there. In 1789–90 I signed the Society's address and its memorial to Congress, and wrote the Historicus satire.
In their words: "Slavery is such an atrocious debasement of human nature, that its very extirpation, if not performed with solicitous care, may sometimes open a source of serious evils." [S501]
Outcome: My public antislavery came late. By Waldstreicher's account I owned slaves until about 1781, and as Society president declined to raise slavery at the 1787 Convention [S406]. Bruce notes the irony [S404].
Use for: when your own practice lags behind the principle you now preach; late change and how others will judge it.
Sources: [S404, S403, S501, S305, S406]

## C17 · 1784 · The Cape May cap
Situation: Benjamin Vaughan asked what remedy I had for the growing luxury of my country, which offended English travellers.
What they did: I said I had no remedy and doubted the evil was as great as claimed. I told how my wife's gift of a fashionable cap set the girls of Cape May knitting mittens to sell, so they could buy caps of their own.
In their words: "Is not the Hope of one day being able to purchase and enjoy Luxuries a great Spur to Labour and Industry?" [S108]
Outcome: A considered view: sumptuary laws cannot be enforced in a great country, and a fool's shilling is picked up by a wiser man. Thrift remained my advice to individuals, not a law for nations.
Use for: whether to ban or tax a 'frivolous' expense in a team, family or country; spending as a spur to effort.
Sources: [S108]

## C18 · 1787 · Signing what I did not fully approve
Situation: The Convention's final text had parts I opposed. Some delegates would not sign. Its reception depended on looking unanimous.
What they did: I gave a closing speech asking every objector to doubt his own infallibility and sign. I agreed to the document "with all its faults", and promised to keep my objections inside the room.
In their words: "The opinions I have had of its errors I sacrifice to the public good." [S205]
Outcome: The closing formula 'Done in Convention by the Unanimous Consent' was then agreed and added. A year later I wrote that the wisest must agree to some unreasonable things to get reasonable ones of more consequence [S205].
Use for: whether to sign, endorse or vote for an imperfect compromise; when to keep your dissent private.
Sources: [S205]
